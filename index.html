Teacher · 8 minutes

Ask: “If something appears on a website, does that mean it must be true?” Invite examples of inaccurate, old or misleading content. Introduce the four checks below.

Introduction · 8 minutes

Four checks for online information

WHO?Who created this information? Do they have relevant knowledge?
WHEN?When was it published or updated? Does the date matter?
EVIDENCE?Does the source explain how it knows?
COMPARE?Do other trustworthy, independent sources agree?

A professional-looking website is not automatically accurate. These checks help you make an informed judgement.

Activity 1 · 10 minutes

Which source would you trust?

You're researching an animal for a science project. Read these fictional search results and choose the strongest starting point.

Teacher prompt: Ask pupils why a named author and supporting evidence matter. Explain that source A is the best starting point, but its claims should still be checked.
Activity 2 · 12 minutes

The conflicting facts investigation

Website A says an animal can live for 25 years. Website B says it can live for 50 years. Both websites look professional.

How would you investigate the difference? Write a short plan with at least two checks.

Discuss: Check authors and evidence; look at dates; consult an independent reliable source; consider whether the sites discuss different species or circumstances. A third website isn't automatically correct.
Activity 3 · 10 minutes

Research, don't just copy

Read this short information passage:

Octopuses have eight arms and are known for their ability to solve problems. They can use their arms to explore objects and open some containers. Different octopus species live in many ocean habitats.

Now explain the main idea in your own words, without copying a complete sentence.

Why can copying the first website you find cause problems?
Teacher: Discuss understanding, reliability, acknowledgement and the difference between paraphrasing and copying. The passage is a teaching example, not a research source.
Plenary · 5 minutes

Exit reflection

Name two checks you will use before trusting an unfamiliar website.

Introduction · 8 minutes

What is a digital footprint?

A digital footprint is information and traces associated with someone's activities online. Some information is shared deliberately; some is collected when services are used.

✉️ Information we sharePosts, comments, uploaded photographs and profile details.
🖱️ Information collectedSome websites and apps record activity, usage or device information.

A digital footprint isn't always negative. It can also reflect creativity, kindness and useful achievements.

Activity 1 · 10 minutes

Footprint detective

For each activity, decide whether it could contribute to a digital footprint.

Teacher: Explain that browsing or playing online may generate records even when a child hasn't posted anything publicly. Printed books and paper drawings do not themselves create online traces.
Activity 2 · 12 minutes

Before you share

You take a funny photograph of your friend at break. You want to share it in a class group chat, but your friend hasn't seen the photograph.

Think about who could see it, how your friend might feel, and what you should do first.

Teacher discussion: Ask permission first; respect a refusal; consider forwarding and screenshots; explain that people can withdraw consent for future sharing even though copies may remain elsewhere.
Activity 3 · 10 minutes

Digital decision challenge

Choose the safest and most respectful response to each situation.

Teacher: Discuss reasons, not just answers. Encourage pupils to seek help from a trusted adult when something online feels unsafe or uncomfortable.
